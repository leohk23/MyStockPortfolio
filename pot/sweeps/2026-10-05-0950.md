## 1. Where the world is

**Observation.** The OECD's September interim outlook says the energy shock has so far been cushioned by alternative supply routes, inventory drawdowns including co-ordinated strategic releases, extra production outside the Gulf and lower Chinese oil demand. It projects global growth of 2.9% in 2026 and G20 inflation of 4.1%, while warning that the buffers are being depleted; [local market data](../../prices.json), updated 4 October, still has WTI at $89.94, up 45.79% over one year. Source: [OECD Interim Economic Outlook press release](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/09/global-growth-holds-up-despite-successive-shocks-but-risks-persist.html). **Inference.** This is resilience purchased with finite buffers, not a clean disinflationary recovery. Businesses exposed to energy and funding costs still need pricing power and cash conversion, while a thesis that relies only on oil rising further is late and fragile.

**Observation.** UNIDO's newly published Q2 data shows global manufacturing production up 1.5% and manufacturing exports up 6.1%; higher-technology production rose 2.0% and exports 7.1%, with Asia-Pacific leading exports and Europe slowest. Source: [UNIDO World Manufacturing Production and Trade, Q2 2026](https://stat.unido.org/publications/qiip). **Inference.** The world is not in a broad factory contraction, but trade and high technology are running well ahead of physical production. That argues against treating every industrial name as a cyclical rebound and raises the bar for companies whose growth is really acquisition, inventory or price rather than volume.

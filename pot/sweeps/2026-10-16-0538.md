**Observation.** BIS Bulletin 138, published on 15 October, finds that disruption at a critical trade node is associated with roughly a 50% larger fall in global industrial-production growth than disruption elsewhere, while downstream producer prices rise about 70 basis points within a year. Electronics and basic metals, including rare earths, are among the hardest inputs to substitute. Japan cut China's share of its rare-earth imports from about 80% to 45% in 2012–14, but paid materially more during the transition. **Inference.** The investable question is not merely whether supply chains will diversify. It is whether an operating supplier outside the concentrated node can turn the higher cost of resilience into durable free cash flow without shareholders funding every expansion. That points to established production and separation assets, not pre-revenue developers.

## 3. Candidates that deserve deeper work

### Lynas Rare Earths — `LYC.AX`, ASX — new candidate

**Why it deserves work.** Lynas adds Australia and Basic Materials to a US- and technology-heavy book, and it already mines and separates rare earths rather than promising future production. FY26 sales volume rose 10.5%, operating cash flow was A$318.8 million and capital/development spending was A$178.3 million. That is real operating evidence behind the non-China supply-chain thesis. The candidate has been added to `watchlist.json`; no outside price or valuation multiple was adopted, so the normal pipeline must establish those before any valuation conclusion.

**What must be true.** FY26's profit step-up must survive something less favourable than a 59.4% rise in average selling price. Cost of sales rose 37.2%, new facilities are still ramping, and the A$1.21 billion cash-and-deposit balance was funded mainly by an approximately A$932 million equity raise rather than retained cash alone. The 75-32-3 check is therefore unfavourable until filings show that higher volumes, product mix and lower unit costs produce durable free cash flow and acceptable returns on the new capital without repeated dilution. Government-backed floor-price agreements may reduce downside, but they cannot substitute for competitive unit economics. This is a candidate for Deep-dive work, not an order.

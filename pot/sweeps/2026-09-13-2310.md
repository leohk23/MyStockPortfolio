## 1. Where the world is

**Observation.** The shock has moved out of the commodity price and into the accounts. Two companies
I looked at closely this run both report a trailing P/E that is not built on trailing operations.
NIKE's fiscal 2026 diluted EPS of **$2.10** contains a **$986m** IEEPA tariff recovery worth about
**$0.52 a share**, which lifted fourth-quarter gross margin by roughly 900 basis points and turned
Q4 net income up 407% on revenue that fell 1%
([Q4 FY26 release, filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320187/000032018726000076/q4fy26exhibit991er.htm)).
CK Infrastructure's half-year profit of **HK$21,252m, up 389%**, is attributed by the company to
"sizeable gains generated from the divestments of UK Power Networks and UK Rails", and prints
**HK$8.43** of half-year EPS
([2026 interim results announcement](https://www.cki.com.hk/english/PDF_file/announcement/2026/20260812_1.pdf)).

**Inference.** A year with an input shock this large produces an unusual crop of one-offs — tariff
refunds, insurance recoveries, disposals into a still-open bid market — and every one of them lands
in the reported EPS this repo divides prices by. `§6.1` fires on the cheapest decile of a name's own
five-year multiple history, so a flattered denominator does not just mislabel a stock as cheap, it
can fire the signal. The machinery for this exists (`normEpsOwn`, `pot/adjustments.json`,
`epsBasis`) but only carries what a deep dive has written into it, which today means pot names.
Both figures above are exactly the sort that need recording before either name is priced locally.
This is also where the Djukanovic reading lands in practice: the argument is about denominators, and
the denominator here is earnings rather than sales.

**Observation.** WTI is **$100.05, +74.55% year to date and +18.4% in the past month**, while the US
10-year yield is **4.975%** and the 5-year **4.791%** ([prices.json](../../prices.json)). Going
outside the block: the World Bank's commodity team, on 17 June, forecast Australian thermal coal
averaging **$130/mt in 2026, up 20% year on year, then falling 12% in 2027**, and expects global
thermal coal demand to stay **roughly flat**, with rises in China and India offsetting European
decline ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/opendata/coal-s-unexpected-comeback--middle-east-conflict-drives-a-return)).
Separately, S&P Dow Jones Indices announced on 4 September that **NIKE, Honeywell Aerospace, Simon
Property and Colgate-Palmolive leave the S&P 100 before the open on 21 September**, replaced by Dell,
Palo Alto Networks, Arista and SanDisk
([SPDJI](https://press.spglobal.com/2026-09-04-Bloom-Energy,-Illumina,-and-Everpure-Set-to-Join-S-P-500-Others-to-Join-S-P-100,-S-P-MidCap-400,-and-S-P-SmallCap-600)).

**Inference.** Three things follow, and the second is the one that changed my mind mid-run.

1. The gas-to-coal switching story reads well in the trade press and badly in the forecast. Flat
   demand and a price the World Bank expects to fall next year is a spot-margin bet, which is the
   7532.T mistake in commodity form — the same conclusion the 18:18 sweep reached about integrated
   oil, arrived at down a different road. The book's Energy weight sits inside the **0.76% residual
   tail** with WTI up three quarters this year, and that gap stays open rather than being closed
   with a name I cannot defend at mid-cycle.
2. The reflex response to an inflation shock is to buy inflation-linked regulated utilities. The
   most experienced listed infrastructure allocator in Hong Kong did the opposite this year: CKI
   **sold** two UK regulated networks and holds **HK$33.9bn net cash**, saying it is "in a stronger
   vantage position to study any potential acquisitions" (same announcement). One company's
   judgement is not evidence, but it is a better-informed judgement than the reflex.
3. All four S&P 100 replacements are information technology. Leo already owns DELL and watches PANW,
   so the index is concentrating in the direction his book already leans. That is not an argument
   against either name; it is an argument that the diversification bias in this brief is describing
   a market-wide drift, not just his own.

### The business

Rolls-Royce makes large jet engines for widebody airliners: the Trent family on the Airbus A350 and A330neo and the Boeing 787. It earns most of its civil profit after the sale. Airlines pay for each hour an engine flies under long-term service agreements (LTSAs), and Rolls-Royce maintains the engine. It also makes engines for military aircraft and for the UK's submarines (Defence), and diesel and gas engines and generators through mtu (Power Systems). Power Systems is increasingly selling into data-centre power: today's update says gas-engine orders *"nearly tripled year on year driven by prime power demand from data centres"*, with a hyperscaler contract being finalised.

Contract catch-ups move profit when cost or durability assumptions change, and the LTSA balance releases cash ahead of the work. A falsifier on revenue, operating profit or guidance would not move while that unwinds. The observables that would move are dated and in the filings: **net LTSA balance growth** each half (+£86m in H1 2026 against +£472m), **contract catch-ups as a share of Civil Aerospace profit**, and **free cash flow against underlying operating profit** (£1,964m against £2,534m in H1 2026). - **Secondary:** Trent XWB-97 durability, with the improvements *"on track to be certified by the end of next year"*, and the remaining Trent 1000 fleet (*"approximately 60%"* upgraded to the XE standard). A slip would raise shop visits under fixed-price LTSAs. Large-engine flying hours are running at the lower end of guidance. And the 2028 targets are being revisited from a starting point already at their floor, so a reset upward is what the price assumes.

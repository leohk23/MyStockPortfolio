### The business

Rolls-Royce makes large jet engines for widebody airliners: the Trent family on the Airbus A350 and A330neo and the Boeing 787. It earns most of its civil profit after the sale. Airlines pay for each hour an engine flies under long-term service agreements (LTSAs), and Rolls-Royce maintains the engine. It also makes engines for military aircraft and for the UK's submarines (Defence), and diesel and gas engines and generators through mtu (Power Systems). Power Systems is increasingly selling into data-centre power: today's update says gas-engine orders *"nearly tripled year on year driven by prime power demand from data centres"*, with a hyperscaler contract being finalised.

**§2.4b does not apply.** Reported revenue grew 16.2% a year over 2022–25, and operating profit rose in the latest year. The releases I read give no split of organic growth into price and volume. The closest volume measure is large-engine flying hours, up 4% year on year in the first ten months of 2026, to 114% of 2019 levels. The company expects the full year *"towards the lower end of the range of 115%–120% of 2019 levels"* (trading update).

**The high margin is a question here, and it is the right one to ask.** A 22.5% margin in a business that earned 5.8% four years ago is either a durable repricing of the LTSA book or a peak. The H1 release gives one sign of which. The net LTSA balance (cash received from airlines ahead of the work) grew only **£86m in H1 2026, against £472m in H1 2025**, which the company attributes to higher shop-visit volumes and supply-chain costs, partly offset by higher LTSA margins and contract catch-ups. Free cash flow has run above profit partly because airlines pay before the shop visit. As shop visits catch up with flying hours, that support fades.

### The 7532.T question

That mistake was a rebound story bought without the fundamentals, the valuation or the currency. Rolls-Royce is the opposite case in every respect but one. The recovery is in the accounts: margin, ROIC and cash have all moved, and guidance was raised and then reaffirmed. The currency exposure is hedged, and the hedge is why the reported EPS jumps about. The one respect in which it repeats the mistake is the one that matters here: buying it now would mean paying for the rebound after it has been priced. The market is not missing the turnaround. The share's 3.46-fold rise since April 2024, against 1.77-fold underlying EPS growth, is the market recording it.

Contract catch-ups move profit when cost or durability assumptions change, and the LTSA balance releases cash ahead of the work. A falsifier on revenue, operating profit or guidance would not move while that unwinds. The observables that would move are dated and in the filings: **net LTSA balance growth** each half (+£86m in H1 2026 against +£472m), **contract catch-ups as a share of Civil Aerospace profit**, and **free cash flow against underlying operating profit** (£1,964m against £2,534m in H1 2026). Next readings are on 25 February 2027.
- **Secondary:** Trent XWB-97 durability, with the improvements *"on track to be certified by the end of next year"*, and the remaining Trent 1000 fleet (*"approximately 60%"* upgraded to the XE standard). A slip would raise shop visits under fixed-price LTSAs. Large-engine flying hours are running at the lower end of guidance. And the 2028 targets are being revisited from a starting point already at their floor, so a reset upward is what the price assumes.
